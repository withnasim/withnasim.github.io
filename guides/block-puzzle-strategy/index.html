How block puzzles actually work: the three shapes that decide a run

Most people lose a block puzzle in the first ninety seconds and spend the next twenty minutes looking for someone to blame. The game is not unfair. The problem is that a block puzzle is a packing problem wearing the costume of a casual game, and packing problems punish the order of your decisions rather than the speed of them.

The board is not the enemy. The gap is.

Every block puzzle gives you three pieces at a time, and those pieces have different shapes: single squares, two by one strips, three by one lines, L shapes, and full three by three squares. The temptation is to place each piece wherever it fits right now. That works for about ten moves. After that your board stops being a grid with some blocks in it and becomes a grid with holes in it.

A hole is a single empty square surrounded on four sides. Nothing except a single square can fill it, and you only draw a single square every so often. Every hole you create is a piece you have wasted. The rule that follows is simple and uncomfortable: place pieces to keep the empty space rectangular, not to clear a line.

Line clears are a symptom, not a strategy

New players chase a clear every turn and end up shuffling the same blocks around. Experienced players clear when the clear also tidies the shape of what is left. That means sometimes you should not clear a full row, because doing so leaves two thin columns you cannot use.

The useful question before a clear is not "can I clear this row" but "what does the board look like after I do". If the answer is a shape with thin spikes, wait one more turn. The board can usually absorb one more piece without dying.

Three by three squares are the whole game

The largest piece is the one that ends most runs, so it should decide your layout from the first move, not the last. Keep a corner reserved for it. That corner does not need to be empty; it needs to be a square of empty space, three by three, that you can free with one or two moves.

When the big square arrives and there is no place for it, the game is over even though the screen still says you have moves left. Watch strong players and you will see them maintaining a running shape rather than a running score.

A practical opening routine

  • Clear toward the centre first. Keep the edges available for long pieces.
  • Leave one edge for the three by one line. Lines need a run of three, and an edge gives you a straight path.
  • Never let a single square stay isolated for more than two turns. Spend a piece to reconnect the space even if it costs points now.
  • Count the next piece before you commit. Placing the first of three pieces well and the second badly is the same as placing all three badly.

Why the score climbs late, not early

Scoring in most block puzzles multiplies with combos and consecutive clears. That is why a careful player overtakes a fast player around the two minute mark: the careful player still has room to make multi line clears, and the fast player is down to single squares and one column gaps.

If you want a number to aim at, aim at board shape rather than points. A board with one clean three by three space and no isolated holes will keep making clears for another five minutes. A board that looks busy but has no usable square is one bad piece away from the end.

Reading a board at a glance

Before you touch a piece, look at three things in this order: the largest empty square, the number of single square holes, and the longest empty run along an edge.

A board with a three by three square and no holes is healthy even if it looks crowded. A board with plenty of empty squares but split into thin strips is already dead, it just has not told you yet. If the best empty run is two squares, a three by one piece will force you to break something.

This check takes three seconds and replaces most of the guessing. Pair it with the rule that you never place a piece into the middle of a clean space, always against an edge or an existing block, and your average run roughly doubles.

Frequently asked

Is the piece order random or generated?

In Brickwise it is drawn, not chosen to punish you. Which means a bad run can always be traced back to two or three specific placements, and watching a replay of your own board is genuinely useful.

Does clearing faster score more?

No. Combos and consecutive clears score more. Speed without shape produces small clears and a full board; shape without speed produces fewer, larger clears, and the larger ones win.

What should I do when the board is nearly full?

Stop trying to clear a line and start trying to free a corner. One three by three space can absorb four or five pieces in a row, and the game often survives long enough for the draw to hand you a single square.

How long should a good run last?

Long enough that you stop watching the score. If you are checking the number every ten seconds, the board shape is already gone.

Play one round with one rule only, keep the empty space rectangular, and ignore the score. It will feel slow for about a minute, and then the run will go longer than any run you have had.
