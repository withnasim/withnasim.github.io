Publishing an Android game from a small studio

Putting a game on Google Play is not hard. Putting one there without losing two weeks to a rejection is a different skill, and nobody teaches it. This is the list I wish I had before our first upload.

Decide the account before you write code

There are two developer account types and the choice follows you. An organisation account needs a registered business and a D-U-N-S number, and it costs more. A personal account is cheaper and faster, but if it was created recently Google requires a closed test before you can publish to production at all: a set number of testers, running for a set number of days, with the testers actually installing and using the build.

That requirement has caught more small studios than any policy change I can remember. Plan for it in the schedule, not after the game is finished, because it adds weeks and you cannot shorten it by paying.

The listing is part of the product

Most players decide in about three seconds, and they decide from two things: the icon and the first screenshot. Not the description.

So the first screenshot has to show the game being played, not a logo on a gradient. The second can show a specific moment that is hard to reach. Anything that looks like a stock render reads as a game that does not exist yet. If a screenshot could belong to any app in the category, it is not doing its job.

The short description is ninety characters. Write what the player does, not what the game is. "Match pairs before the cards flip back" beats "A beautiful memory experience".

The two forms that cause most rejections

Privacy policy. You need a reachable URL, and it has to match what the app actually does. A policy that says "we collect nothing" next to an analytics SDK is the fastest way to a rejection, and it is also a lie that is easy to check.

Data safety. This form is not marketing. If your game has an ad SDK, that SDK collects an advertising identifier, and the form has to say so. Declaring less than you ship is the single most common cause of a takedown after launch, and it can arrive months later when you have forgotten the form exists.

Ads, families, and the declaration

If the game shows ads, declare it. If it is aimed at children, the rules tighten: ads have to be appropriate, and personalised advertising is not allowed for child directed apps. That is why Peep has no behavioural ads at all. Getting this wrong is not a warning, it is removal from the families programme.

Target API level is a clock, not a task

Google raises the minimum target API level every year, and apps that fall behind stop being available to new users. If you publish once and walk away, your game quietly disappears from search. The fix is boring: one update a year, scheduled, even if the update only changes the target level.

What to measure, and what to ignore

Ignore total installs for the first month. They are mostly noise and they mostly come from your own sharing.

Watch three numbers instead. Day one retention, because if people do not come back the next day nothing else matters. Crash free rate, because a crash on the first open is the last open. And install source, because it tells you whether the store found your players or your own posting did.

Day seven retention matters more than day one for a puzzle, but it takes a week to arrive, which is why most people stop looking before they see it.

Reviews, honestly

Answer the negative ones with a specific fix, not a thank you. "The level 40 puzzle was unsolvable, fixed in today's update, thank you" is worth ten five star reviews. Never argue, never explain the design to a player who is already annoyed, and never buy reviews. The store removes them and the account carries the mark.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to publish?

The developer account is a one time fee, and there is no per download charge. The real cost is time: the closed test period, the store assets, and the yearly target API update.

Do I need a company?

Not to publish as an individual. You need an organisation account only if you want the store listing to carry a business name, or if you are handling payments in a way that requires it.

Can I publish the same game on iOS?

Yes, but treat it as a separate project. The review guidelines differ, and the screenshots, privacy declarations and testing requirements are not interchangeable.

What if my first game fails?

It probably will, and that is normal. The second one is cheaper to build because the account, the assets and the process are already done. Most small studios find their game on the third or fourth attempt.
