Why we made Peep offline and free

Peep is a talking pet. You tap the chick, it says something back, and children find that funny for far longer than adults do. Almost every app in that category is built the same way: the reply is generated on a server, which means every tap costs the developer money and every player needs a working internet connection. We built Peep the other way round, and this is why.

What a talking pet costs to run

A server generated reply is cheap per call and expensive per player. Each tap sends a request, the server answers, the voice is synthesised, and the audio comes back. Multiply that by a child who taps forty times in ten minutes and you have a product whose running cost grows with its own success.

That cost has to be paid by something. In most apps it is paid by advertising density, by a subscription, or by collecting data that can be sold or used to target ads. Each of those choices makes the product worse for a five year old.

The player we were actually designing for

Most of our players are in Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Indonesia and the Philippines. Three things are true for many of them at once: mobile data is bought in small packages, the connection drops in the middle of the day, and the phone is often shared with a parent or an older sibling.

A game that needs the network to answer a chicken is a game that fails at exactly the wrong moment. A child does not understand buffering. They understand that it stopped working, and they stop opening it.

What offline actually changes

When the replies live inside the app, the game opens instantly, works on a bus with no signal, and costs nothing per tap. It also means we can make a promise that server based apps cannot: the game does not need to know anything about the child to work.

The trade off is real and we accepted it. Offline replies are written, not generated, so Peep has a fixed personality rather than an endlessly inventive one. A child will hear the same joke twice. In exchange, the app works every time, and nothing is uploaded anywhere.

The safety decisions behind it

  • No account, no sign in, no profile. There is nothing to leak because there is nothing collected.
  • No chat, no text input that leaves the device, no strangers.
  • No external links. A misplaced tap cannot take a child to a browser.
  • No behavioural advertising on child directed screens. Where advertising appears at all, it is kept away from the pet itself.
  • No data collection beyond anonymous crash reports, which contain nothing about the player.

These are not marketing points. They are the reason the app could be built offline in the first place: a game that does not need a server does not need to identify anyone.

What we cut, and why the game got better

The first build had a larger vocabulary and a system that tried to react to what the child tapped. It felt clever in testing with adults and confusing with children, because the pet kept answering questions nobody had asked. We cut it back to a smaller set of clear reactions: the chick responds to being tapped, being fed, being ignored and being put to sleep.

The smaller version got better laughs, which is the only metric that matters for a toy. It also got faster, because there was less to load.

What this means for the studio

Peep is free, and it stays free. It has no purchases. Running it costs us almost nothing, which is exactly why we can keep the promise that most free children's apps quietly break.

If you are building something similar, the lesson we would pass on is this: decide what your app must do without the network, then build outward from there. The rest of the features are optional. The first minute of a five year old's attention is not.

Questions we get asked

Does Peep need internet at all?

No. It downloads once from the store and then runs on the device. Flight mode makes no difference to how it behaves.

How do updates work if nothing talks to a server?

Through the store, the same way any app updates. We ship new lines and reactions as a version update rather than remotely, which also means a version you already have cannot be changed underneath you.

Why does the chick repeat itself?

Because the replies are written and finite. A generated pet can be endlessly varied and needs a server for every tap. We chose the version that works on a bus with no signal, and repetition is the honest cost of that choice.

Are there ads in the game?

Peep is not a place we put behavioural advertising. There is nothing to target, because the app does not know who is playing.
